LOOKING AHEAD · FOR ATHLETES & FAMILIES · 3 MIN

Could running be
part of college?

It might be. But middle school is a good time to enjoy the sport, build a broad foundation, and keep plenty of doors open—not to feel recruited.

IN THIS ARTICLEDifferent college pathsWhat matters nowExplore when the time comes

Could a middle school runner one day run in college? Possibly. But there is no need to have the whole path mapped out now. College is years away; the useful thing today is to build a healthy relationship with running and stay curious about what comes next.

There is more than one way to keep running

Some colleges have varsity cross country and track & field teams. NCAA schools compete in Divisions I, II, or III, with different athletics and campus experiences. At other schools, students may find a running club. And some people simply keep running on their own or with friends. These options are not interchangeable—and a particular school may not offer every one.

Athletic aid rules differ too. NCAA guidance says Division I and II schools may offer athletics scholarships; Division III schools do not. Other kinds of financial aid may be available, depending on a student and school. Those details can change, so families should check current NCAA information and ask each college directly.

KEEP THE OPTIONS OPEN

“College running” can mean a varsity roster, a campus club, or simply continuing to run for enjoyment alongside college life. A team is not the only measure of success.

What matters right now?

For a Grades 6–8 athlete, college recruiting is not the assignment. Middle school is a chance to try events, learn good habits, enjoy being part of a group, and figure out whether running is something they want to keep doing.

  • Try different kinds of running and other activities.
  • Learn to listen, be a good teammate, and ask questions.
  • Keep school, friends, rest, and sport in the same picture.
  • Let the athlete’s interests—not outside pressure—set the pace.
FOR PARENTS & ATHLETES

Try asking “What did you enjoy about practice?” before “How fast did you run?” It keeps the conversation about the person, not only the performance.

When the time comes

Later—when high school and college choices are closer—students can look at schools that fit academically and personally, then check whether those schools have a team or club that matches their interests. A high-school coach or counselor can help families sort through questions. NCAA eligibility and recruiting rules are detailed and can change, so use the official NCAA Eligibility Center rather than old social posts or generic recruiting charts.

This camp is about preparing for high school track and cross country—not promising college admission, recruitment, a roster spot, or a scholarship. The next step is the one right in front of the athlete.
