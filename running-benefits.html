WELLBEING · FOR ATHLETES & FAMILIES · 3 MIN

Why running can
be good for you.

Running is one way to move your body. The best reasons to keep at it may have less to do with a stopwatch—and more to do with how you feel, learn, and grow.

IN THIS ARTICLEWhat movement can supportMake it enjoyableKeep a healthy balance

Some runs feel effortless. Some feel like you are counting every lamppost. And sometimes the best part is the person running beside you. Running is not magic—but when it is enjoyable and right for the person doing it, it can add something good to an ordinary day.

A little movement goes a long way

The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention says regular physical activity supports health and fitness in children and teens. Active young people tend to have higher fitness levels and stronger bones and muscles. The CDC also describes brain-health benefits, including better cognition and memory, and reduced symptoms of depression.

That does not mean a run guarantees better grades, fixes a hard day, or works the same for everyone. It simply gives families a good reason to make enjoyable movement part of life—alongside sleep, friendships, school, and downtime.

THE BROADER GUIDELINE

For ages 6–17, U.S. guidance recommends 60 minutes or more of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity a day. That is total activity across the day—not a daily running target. The CDC encourages activities that are varied, age-appropriate, and enjoyable.

The best run is not always the fastest

For a middle school runner, a win might be finding a comfortable rhythm, learning a new route, finishing a tough practice with a friend, or feeling a little less nervous at the starting line. A personal best can be exciting, but it is only one way to measure a season.

A QUESTION TO TAKE WITH YOU

After your next run, ask: “What felt better than I expected?” Not every answer needs to be a time.

  • Choose movement that feels interesting and age-appropriate.
  • Make room for rest, school, friends, and other interests.
  • Celebrate consistency, curiosity, and effort—not only results.
  • Tell a parent or trusted adult when something does not feel right.

Keep running in its place

Young athletes are still growing, and every runner is different. No article can decide what is right for one person’s body or health. For individual concerns, families should talk with a qualified health professional. A supportive approach leaves space for rest and for a young person to enjoy sport on their own terms.

Running does not have to lead anywhere in particular. It can just be a way to move, learn, spend time with others, and see what you are capable of today. That is enough.
